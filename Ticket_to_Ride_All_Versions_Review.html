The Complete Ticket to Ride
Atlas & Ranking

Twelve canonical maps — from Alan R. Moon's original 1910 North American crossing to the Daviau/Leacock Legends of the West legacy campaign. What each board actually does to the game, how its rules reshape the decision space, and where each earns its place on the shelf.

CATEGORY Family Competitive Strategy (Cat. 3) DESIGNER Alan R. Moon (2004–) PUBLISHER Days of Wonder RANKING LENS Strategic depth · Aesthetic & production

How the maps actually differ

Every Ticket to Ride shares the same elegant spine: draw cards, claim routes, complete tickets. What the maps vary is far more interesting — they change the shape of the decision. Here's the analytical framework I use to compare them.

Map geometry

Routes per city, parallel options, bottleneck density. Dense parallel networks (USA) reward tempo; sparse networks (UK, Nordic) punish a missed ticket.

The rule twist

What each map layers on top: tunnels, ferries, passengers, stations, terrain cards, tolls, tech trees, ships, legacy unlocks. This is where strategic depth lives.

Interaction pressure

How often you step on each other's plans. A board with few redundant routes turns social — the classic Rochat table tension.

Aesthetic grounding

Illustration, palette, period authenticity. Most Ticket to Ride boards are handsome; a few (Legends of the West, Märklin, India) are genuinely beautiful objects.

The canonical set — twelve maps, reviewed

Every major standalone or map-collection release that significantly alters play. City micro-games (New York, London, Berlin, Paris, San Francisco), kids' editions, and minor expansions are deliberately set aside.

2004 · The Origin

Ticket to Ride

The cross-continental North American map — 1910

2–5 players 30–60 min BGG weight 1.84 Age 8+

The twist

None — and that's the point. Pure route-building in its cleanest form, with one coast-to-coast longest-route bonus that shapes the whole game.

What it does to the table

Wide, generous map with many parallel routes. Everyone can follow their own plan for the first half; tension arrives late, when someone hoards the last Chicago–Denver link. Low conflict, high onboarding. This is the version for teaching a five-year-old and a fifty-year-old at the same table.

Aesthetic

Julien Delval's original illustration is warm sepia Americana. The 10th and 15th Anniversary editions (with painted metal trains and fabric ticket pouches) are the only USA versions worth collecting as objects.

2005 · The Evolution

Ticket to Ride: Europe

Edinburgh to Constantinople — the turn-of-the-century grand tour

2–5 players 30–60 min BGG weight 1.95 Age 8+

The twist

Three additions change everything: tunnels (claim cost is probabilistic — you may need to pay extra cards), ferries (require at least one locomotive wild), and train stations (pay to piggy-back on a rival's route to complete your ticket). Also: longest route renamed to longest continuous rail, still game-deciding.

What it does to the table

Stations neutralise the cruelty of getting blocked — the game becomes less zero-sum, more negotiable. Tunnels add a satisfying push-your-luck moment. Ferries force you to bank locomotives early. The net effect: slightly more strategic friction, more forgiving of new players. This is the version most collectors quietly consider the "correct" Ticket to Ride.

Aesthetic

Julien Delval again, but richer — borders, illuminated cities, a 1912-ish sepia that ages beautifully. The 15th Anniversary edition (2021) is the collector's pick, with larger cards and cloth bag.

Gateway-plus Stations soften blocking 15th Anniversary available

★★★★☆Aesthetic
★★★★★Replayability
2006 (as Märklin) · 2012 reissue as Germany

Ticket to Ride: Märklin / Germany

Two billion annual rail passengers — now on the board

2–5 players 30–60 min BGG weight 2.3 Age 8+

The twist

The passenger meeple system. Cities hold stacks of passenger tokens representing trade goods; when you complete a route you move a passenger along your network and collect points from every city they pass through. The result: routes have two values — the base train score, plus a passenger-harvesting dimension.

What it does to the table

This is the deepest of the "still teachable in five minutes" versions. You now choose routes on three criteria (card matching, your tickets, and passenger value), and the order you complete them in genuinely matters — you harvest passengers once. Interaction spikes because everyone's eyeing the same juicy big-city stacks (Berlin, München). A serious strategist's Ticket to Ride that still welcomes newcomers.

Aesthetic

The original 2006 Märklin edition co-branded with the legendary German model-train firm — its box included upgraded passenger meeples and was a genuine object. The 2012 Germany reissue kept the rules but lost the Märklin polish. Track down the original Märklin for the Artisan shelf.

2007 · The Tight Two-Player

Ticket to Ride: Nordic Countries

Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Finland — designed for 2–3 players only

2–3 players 30–60 min BGG weight 2.0 Age 8+

The twist

Tunnels and ferries carry over from Europe, but with a sharp rule change: locomotives are wild only on tunnel and ferry routes. On a regular track, a locomotive is dead weight. This forces you into the mountainous north and the Baltic crossings rather than avoiding them.

What it does to the table

Because the map is designed for exactly 2–3 players, every route matters and the blocking is brutal. Unlike the generous USA map, there is usually one viable way to complete a given ticket. This is the Ticket to Ride that plays like a chess problem. The best two-player TtR ever made; the go-to after-dinner game for two.

Aesthetic

Cold palette, lighthouse illustrations, Nordic typography. Handsome but not ornate — genuinely evokes the fjords.

2–3 player only Tight, cutthroat

2011 · Map Collection Vol. 1

Ticket to Ride: Asia

Two maps — Team Asia (teams of two) and Legendary Asia (solo across mountains)

2–6 players 30–60 min BGG weight 2.2 Requires USA or Europe base

The twist

Team Asia is the only officially supported team game in the series — 4 or 6 players split into pairs with a shared hand/private hand split. Legendary Asia adds mountain routes (costing an extra piece that is set aside permanently) — a route-scarcity mechanic that compounds over the game.

What it does to the table

Team Asia is the rare version that works at 6 players without fragmenting the table — the whispered hand-coordination is genuinely fun and novel. Legendary Asia feels punishing because you're losing trains, not just spending them — every Himalayan crossing is a real cost decision. The Team format alone justifies the box for larger family gatherings.

Aesthetic

Illustrator Julien Delval signature style — warm, atmospheric, with elephants and mountain passes. Less iconic than Europe but period-appropriate.

Team play (4–6) Mountain routes Needs base game

2011 · Map Collection Vol. 2

Ticket to Ride: India (& Switzerland)

The Mandala bonus — elegant geometric scoring

2–5 players (India) 2–3 players (Switzerland) 30–60 min Requires base game

The twist

India introduces Mandalas: earn a bonus if you complete a destination ticket via two different non-overlapping routes. It rewards network redundancy — the opposite of every other Ticket to Ride instinct. The flip-side Switzerland map (designed for 2–3 players) restricts locomotive wilds to tunnels, much like Nordic.

What it does to the table

The Mandala rule is genuinely novel — it creates the only TtR map where you're happy about drawing duplicate tickets, and where spending extra turns reinforcing an existing connection is correct play. It also defuses some of the brutality: aggressive blocking is less punishing because you can loop around. A subtle, mathematically elegant twist.

Aesthetic

Ian Vincent's illustration is a standout — palace domes, brightly coloured borders, hand-drawn routes. Genuinely the most beautiful map in the collection alongside Legends of the West.

2012 · Map Collection Vol. 3

Ticket to Ride: The Heart of Africa

Terrain cards — the colour-matching hand becomes a strategic resource

2–5 players 30–60 min BGG weight 2.3 Requires base game

The twist

Terrain cards. Certain routes are designated as crossing Savanna, Jungle, or Mountain terrain. If you play train cards matching the terrain, you score double points on that route. If not, you score normal. You earn terrain cards by drawing from a parallel deck — creating a second currency and a real tension between colour-collecting and terrain-collecting.

What it does to the table

Deceptively rich. Terrain cards triple the decision space on every card draw — do I take that visible blue train, or the terrain card that doubles my jungle routes? Route selection also becomes a puzzle: the same Nairobi–Cairo line is worth vastly different points depending on whose terrain colours you hold. Probably the most strategically interesting single-map release after Märklin. Rewards players who think two steps ahead.

Aesthetic

Vibrant, savanna-yellow board with hand-drawn wildlife illustrations. One of the warmer palettes in the collection.

Terrain card system Deep route-choice layer Needs base game

2013 · Map Collection Vol. 4

Ticket to Ride: Nederland

Bridge tolls — the first "economy" in Ticket to Ride

2–5 players 30–60 min BGG weight 2.6 Requires base game

The twist

Because all of Nederland is canals, every route is a bridge. When you claim a bridge, you pay a toll in coins — equal to the number of trains used. Those coins go to the bank or to opponents who already have routes at that city. End-game bonus: whoever has the most toll revenue wins a big prize; whoever has the least is penalised. A genuine auction/economy layer.

What it does to the table

This is the most "euro-strategy" Ticket to Ride. You're now managing a second resource (coins), and the blocking calculus flips — now you want rivals to build through your city because they pay you. It produces the most negotiation/trash-talk of any Ticket to Ride. For the Rochat table's adversarial energy, this is probably the perfect variant.

Aesthetic

Cool palette of blues and greys, ornate canal illustrations. Less visually striking than India, but historically faithful.

Toll economy High interaction Needs base game

2015 · Map Collection Vol. 5

Ticket to Ride: United Kingdom & Pennsylvania

Technology cards (UK) and railway shares (Pennsylvania)

2–5 players 30–60 min BGG weight 2.6 Requires base game

The twist

The most ambitious rule-stack of any map pack. UK: you can't claim certain routes until you've purchased the appropriate technology card (Steam Engine, Signalling, Brunel's Broad Gauge) — a mini tech-tree that unlocks the map in stages. Pennsylvania: every route you claim gives you a share in one of the regional railway companies; end-game scoring rewards the majority shareholder of each line — a hidden-points layer that rewires what routes are worth taking.

What it does to the table

UK turns Ticket to Ride into a light engine-builder — the first few turns are spent buying tech rather than laying track, which is divisive. Pennsylvania's share-majority scoring is genuinely clever: an opponent's "worthless" one-train route might actually be stealing a majority share worth 30 points at game end. Pennsylvania is arguably the best-balanced map in the series; UK is the most cerebral but the slowest to start.

Aesthetic

UK uses a Victorian engineering motif — brass gears, period cartography. Pennsylvania is folksier, period American. Both boards are handsome if less ornate than India.

Tech tree (UK) Share majorities (PA) Needs base game

2019 · Map Collection Vol. 7

Ticket to Ride: Japan & Italy

Two very different philosophies in one box

2–5 players 30–60 min BGG weight 2.4 Requires base game

The twist

Japan: introduces the Bullet Train, a shared network of high-speed routes that every player can contribute to. At game-end, players who've contributed most to the shared network earn bonuses; those who ignored it are penalised. A cooperative-competitive hybrid. Italy: ferries cross the Mediterranean and connect Sicily/Sardinia; ticket tiers reward you for hitting destinations across multiple regions of the peninsula.

What it does to the table

Japan's shared bullet network is the only TtR mechanic with a true cooperative dimension — you actively help opponents (and hope they overpay for the privilege). It fundamentally changes the social contract. Italy is more conventional but the regional ticket scoring creates nice tension between breadth and depth. Fits Chris's preferences: Italy for historical Mediterranean period charm; Japan for feudal/Meiji-to-modern aesthetics.

Aesthetic

Two distinct art styles in one box: Japan uses ukiyo-e-inspired line work with reds and golds; Italy uses warm renaissance tones. Both strong.

Shared network (JP) Regional bonuses (IT) Two maps, one box

2016 · The Grand Standalone

Ticket to Ride: Rails & Sails

Global map with ocean routes — the biggest box in the series

2–5 players 90–120 min BGG weight 2.9 Age 10+

The twist

Two card decks (trains and ships), double-sided board (World / Great Lakes), and player-purchased harbours that unlock bonus scoring for ship-to-city connections. Ships come in single- and double-value. You can rebalance your starting train/ship ratio. End-game heavily rewards commercial hubs.

What it does to the table

This is the longest, heaviest Ticket to Ride — a full two hours with decisions to match. Ocean crossings have real cost; the World map rewards imperial thinking (Constantinople as hub, Singapore as node, etc). Harbours are where the strategy lives: a well-placed harbour can double your ship points. A legitimate two-hour strategy evening, not a filler.

Aesthetic

Gorgeous world-map illustration with art-deco cartouches. The box is enormous; the table presence is excellent. Arguably the most visually impressive non-legacy edition.

Ships + harbours Standalone (no base needed) 2-hour commitment

2023 · The Legacy Campaign

Ticket to Ride: Legacy — Legends of the West

Moon + Daviau + Leacock, twelve linked sessions, one transformed game

2–5 players 20–90 min per session 12 sessions total Age 10+

The twist

A twelve-session legacy campaign. The map starts small (east coast) and unfurls west across the Mississippi, through the Rockies, to the Pacific. Each session introduces new mechanics via sealed "frontier boxes": money as a second resource, California gold rushes, event cards, transatlantic routes, even earthquakes that destroy rail. Stickers go on the board. Cards get torn up. Between sessions, the temporary leader gets permanent (ish) advantages and the last-place player gets a catch-up boon.

What it does to the table

Legacy games only work with a committed group of the same 3–5 players for 12 sessions — not casual. When it works, the opening-a-new-box ritual is genuinely thrilling and the gradual complexity growth means even hardened players are learning new things in session 9. The co-design pedigree (Daviau = Pandemic Legacy, Risk Legacy; Leacock = Pandemic) shows in the pacing. Best played with the immediate family as a winter-evening ritual, not the strategic circle.

Aesthetic

The production is the richest in the series: illustrated Americana in the style of 1870s frontier chromolithographs, thick card, varnished postcards, a "Conductor's Toolbox" for storing game-state. Easily the most object-like Ticket to Ride ever produced, though once used it's no longer resaleable — that's the legacy bargain.

The ranking — weighted on strategic depth and aesthetic production

Tier lists are opinions with confidence. These are mine, using the lens Chris set: how much does each map's rule twist change the decision space, and how handsome is it as an object on the shelf. Onboarding and casual fit get a secondary nod.

S TIER — The Benchmarks The maps where the rules twist genuinely rewrites how you think about Ticket to Ride
  1. Märklin / Germany Depth benchmark — passenger meeples are the single best rules layer in the series. Adds a whole new scoring axis without complicating onboarding. The Märklin edition is a collector object in its own right.
  2. Legends of the West Aesthetic benchmark — the most beautiful, most narratively ambitious Ticket to Ride ever produced. The legacy format is its own category; nothing else delivers this much novelty, but it's one-and-done.
  3. Nederland Most strategic map pack — the toll economy is the most genuinely euro-strategic rule twist, and the trash-talk-per-hour ratio is the highest in the series. Perfect fit for an adversarial family table.
A TIER — Deep & Beautiful Excellent rule layers and strong aesthetic grounding; any of these deserves shelf space
  1. The Heart of Africa Hidden depth — terrain cards triple the decision space at every card draw. Underrated; arguably deeper than Nederland.
  2. United Kingdom & Pennsylvania Most cerebral — UK's tech tree is polarising; Pennsylvania's share-majority scoring is quietly brilliant. Engineering-era aesthetic suits Chris's period preferences.
  3. Rails & Sails Grandest standalone — the two-hour evening version. Gorgeous world map, ship/train balance gives real strategic width. Stands alone; no base game required.
  4. Europe The defining edition — stations, tunnels, ferries. The version most veterans default to. The 15th Anniversary edition is a serious collector's pick.
  5. Japan & Italy Aesthetic pair — the shared Bullet Train network in Japan is a genuine mechanical novelty; Italy is the better fit with Mediterranean period aesthetics.
B TIER — Specialists & Solid Excellent in their niche, but less essential if you already have the A-tier
  1. Nordic Countries Best 2-player — the definitive couple's Ticket to Ride. Punishingly tight, genuinely replayable, but only at 2–3 players.
  2. India Most beautiful map — Mandala bonus is mathematically elegant and the illustration is gorgeous. Aesthetically an A-tier entry, but the mechanical twist is narrower than the others.
  3. Asia Team play — Team Asia is the only proper team variant in the series. Essential if the extended family-plus-partners count regularly climbs to 6; optional otherwise.
C TIER — The Origin & the Unnecessary Historically important, but mechanically superseded by everything above
  1. Ticket to Ride (USA) The gateway — still the best teaching edition, still a fine family game, but mechanically the thinnest of the lot. The 10th and 15th Anniversary editions (metal trains, fabric bag) are the only versions worth the shelf space if you already own Europe.

Verdict — curation for the Rochat library

The shortlist, if you want the whole family of Ticket to Ride to live on one shelf

The canonical set of twelve is too many for a lifetime collection — most households reach their ceiling at three or four. Given the Rochat family's adversarial-but-fun energy, mixed-experience tables, and taste for historically grounded production, here's the shelf I'd actually build:

Keep — already on the shelf

Ticket to Ride (USA) — the onboarding standard. Upgrade the wooden wagons as you already have. Consider the 15th Anniversary edition down the line for the Artisan shelf.

Acquire first — the strategic upgrade

Märklin edition (hunt on the secondary market) for the passenger meeple system. If unavailable, the Germany reissue. This is the single biggest strategic upgrade available in the series.

Acquire second — two-player & aesthetic

Nordic Countries for the quiet two-player evenings, and India / Switzerland for the Mandala twist plus the most beautiful board in the collection.

Consider — the big table

Nederland if the Rochat adversarial energy wants a toll economy to argue about; Asia if 6-player team nights become a regular thing.

One evening only

Rails & Sails for the annual two-hour "grand tour" evening; Legends of the West as a twelve-session winter ritual with the immediate family — start in December, finish in spring.

Skip, unless a gift

UK / Pennsylvania is excellent but skews cerebral in a way that competes with the Pax Pamir / John Company slot on the shelf. Japan & Italy is beautiful but overlaps with Europe mechanically.

A closing note on aesthetics

None of the Ticket to Ride editions reach Wehrlegig territory — the cloth boards, chalk pieces, and historical footnotes of Pax Pamir are simply a different register. But the series has genuine highs: the original Märklin edition (for co-branded upgraded components), Legends of the West (for production value), Rails & Sails (for illustration), and India (for sheer beauty of board art) all merit the Artisan flag within their category. The 15th Anniversary Europe edition — painted metal trains, cloth ticket pouch, oversized cards — is the closest Ticket to Ride gets to an heirloom object.
