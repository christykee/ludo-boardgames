Before hunting, learn the terrain. The Indian chess tradition is not one tradition but a layered geology of forms — proto-chess from the Gupta court (c.600 CE), Persian abstraction under Islam, Mughal court figural ivory, four distinct colonial export workshops, and the adjacent traditions of Burma, Ceylon, Kashmir, and the Himalayas. I

c. 600 CE · Northern India · Gupta Court

Chaturanga — The Four Limbs of the Army Chaturanga — Les Quatre Bras de l'Armée

The proto-chess. A Sanskrit war game whose four divisions of the Mauryan-Gupta army still survive, fifteen centuries later, as the rook, knight, bishop and pawn. Le proto-échec. Un jeu de guerre sanskrit dont les quatre divisions de l'armée maurya-gupta survivent encore, quinze siècles plus tard, sous la forme de la tour, du cavalier, du fou et du pion.

In the 6th century, in a court somewhere in the Gupta empire — perhaps Pataliputra (modern Patna), perhaps Ujjain — someone took the existing 8×8 grid called the aṣṭāpada, already used for race games on Indian temple floors, and placed onto it a war in miniature. Four divisions of the army: foot soldiers (padāti), cavalry (aśva), elephants (gaja), and chariots (ratha). A king. A counsellor. The Sanskrit name was chaturanga — "four limbs."

No physical sets survive. We know the game from texts — the Bhavishya Purana, the Harshacharita (c.625 CE) of Bāṇabhaṭṭa, and most famously the description in Persian sources after the game crossed the Hindu Kush. Material evidence is limited to carved reliefs at Buddhist sites such as Sanchi, where players appear bent over boards on temple steps. The pieces were almost certainly simple: turned wood, terracotta, bronze.

Why this matters for a collector buying in 2026: every Indian chess set in every auction house descends, conceptually, from this moment. When a 19th-century Berhampur carver gives you a rook in the form of an elephant with a howdah, he is honouring chaturanga. When a Vizagapatam workshop turns a knight as a horse-and-rider rather than the European horse-head alone, he is honouring chaturanga. The set you eventually buy is not an English form decorated in Indian style — it is a chaturanga set filtered through Persian abstraction and English standardisation. The DNA is original.

There is one piece of practical news for the collector: the four-fold army idea also spawned chaturaji, a four-player variant played with dice, and a small number of ivory pieces from this tradition do appear at auction — usually unrecognised, mis-catalogued as "antique Indian chess." If you ever see a set with four sides of pieces in different stains rather than two armies of opposing colours, you may be looking at a chaturaji.

Au VIe siècle, dans une cour quelque part dans l'empire Gupta — peut-être Pataliputra (Patna moderne), peut-être Ujjain — quelqu'un prit la grille 8×8 existante appelée aṣṭāpada, déjà utilisée pour des jeux de course sur les sols des temples indiens, et y plaça une guerre miniature. Quatre divisions de l'armée : fantassins (padāti), cavalerie (aśva), éléphants (gaja), et chars (ratha). Un roi. Un conseiller. Le nom sanskrit était chaturanga — « quatre bras ».

Aucun jeu physique ne survit. Nous connaissons le jeu par les textes — le Bhavishya Purana, le Harshacharita (vers 625) de Bāṇabhaṭṭa, et plus célèbrement par la description dans les sources persanes après que le jeu eut traversé l'Hindou Kouch. Les preuves matérielles se limitent à des reliefs sculptés sur des sites bouddhiques comme Sanchi. Les pièces étaient presque certainement simples : bois tourné, terre cuite, bronze.

Pourquoi cela importe pour le collectionneur en 2026 : chaque jeu d'échecs indien de chaque maison de ventes descend, conceptuellement, de ce moment. Quand un sculpteur de Berhampur du XIXe siècle vous donne une tour sous la forme d'un éléphant avec un howdah, il honore le chaturanga. Quand un atelier de Vizagapatam tourne un cavalier comme un cheval-et-cavalier plutôt que la simple tête de cheval européenne, il honore le chaturanga. Le jeu que vous achèterez n'est pas une forme anglaise décorée à l'indienne — c'est un jeu de chaturanga filtré par l'abstraction persane et la standardisation anglaise. L'ADN est d'origine.

Une nouvelle pratique pour le collectionneur : l'idée des quatre armées engendra aussi le chaturaji, une variante à quatre joueurs jouée avec des dés, et un petit nombre de pièces d'ivoire de cette tradition apparaissent aux enchères — généralement non reconnues, mal cataloguées comme « échecs indiens anciens ». Si vous voyez un jour un jeu à quatre camps de pièces aux teintes différentes plutôt qu'à deux armées opposées, vous regardez peut-être un chaturaji.

II

8th–15th c. · Persia · Caliphate · Al-Andalus

Persian Shatranj — The Aniconic Turn Shatranj Persan — Le Tournant Aniconique

When chaturanga crossed into Sassanid Persia and then into the Islamic world, the figural pieces dissolved into geometry. The horse, the elephant, the chariot — all became abstract turned forms. This is the lineage of every Muslim Staunton you will ever encounter. Quand le chaturanga traversa la Perse sassanide puis le monde islamique, les pièces figuratives se dissolvèrent en géométrie. Le cheval, l'éléphant, le char — tous devinrent des formes abstraites tournées. C'est la lignée de chaque Staunton musulman que vous rencontrerez.

The Sassanid Persians adopted chaturanga around 600 CE and renamed it shatranj — a Persian rendering of the Sanskrit. When the Arab armies overthrew the Sassanid empire (637–651), they absorbed the game and carried it across the entire Islamic world: from Bukhara to Baghdad, from Cairo to Cordoba, eventually reaching Norman Sicily and Christian Spain by the 11th century.

The crucial transformation happened in the workshops of the Caliphate. Islamic theology — particularly its strict aniconic principle, the prohibition on figural representation of living beings — gradually reshaped the chess pieces. The horseman became a slanted disc. The elephant became a ribbed cylinder with two stylised "tusks" projecting outward. The chariot became a small flanged tower. The king and his vizier (the precursor to the queen) became simple turned cones with finials. The pieces were no longer images of an army. They were geometry that referred to an army.

A handful of these early Islamic sets survive — the Charlemagne chessmen at the Cabinet des médailles in Paris (12th–13th c. Egyptian ivory, mistakenly attributed for centuries to a gift from Hārūn al-Rashīd to Charlemagne); the Lewis chessmen in the British Museum, made in Norse Trondheim around 1170 but visually indebted to Islamic abstraction transmitted through Spain; and the celebrated rock-crystal pieces from Fatimid Egypt held by the Met and the Louvre.

For the Indian collector this lineage matters in one specific way: when British Staunton sets reached Islamic India after 1849, the local response — the Muslim Staunton — was not an invention. It was a return. The aniconic vocabulary had been waiting in Persian and Mughal memory for a thousand years.

Les Sassanides adoptèrent le chaturanga vers 600 et le rebaptisèrent shatranj — rendu persan du sanskrit. Quand les armées arabes renversèrent l'empire sassanide (637–651), elles absorbèrent le jeu et le portèrent à travers tout le monde islamique : de Boukhara à Bagdad, du Caire à Cordoue, atteignant la Sicile normande et l'Espagne chrétienne au XIe siècle.

La transformation cruciale eut lieu dans les ateliers du Califat. La théologie islamique — en particulier son strict principe aniconique, l'interdiction de représentation figurative des êtres vivants — remodela progressivement les pièces. Le cavalier devint un disque incliné. L'éléphant devint un cylindre nervuré avec deux « défenses » stylisées projetées vers l'extérieur. Le char devint une petite tour à flasques. Le roi et son vizir devinrent de simples cônes tournés à fleurons. Les pièces n'étaient plus des images d'une armée. Elles étaient une géométrie qui se référait à une armée.

Quelques-uns de ces premiers ensembles islamiques survivent — les échecs de Charlemagne au Cabinet des médailles à Paris (ivoire égyptien, XIIe–XIIIe siècle, attribués à tort pendant des siècles à un cadeau de Hārūn al-Rashīd à Charlemagne) ; les échecs de Lewis au British Museum, faits à Trondheim norroise vers 1170 mais visuellement débiteurs de l'abstraction islamique transmise par l'Espagne ; et les célèbres pièces en cristal de roche de l'Égypte fatimide conservées au Met et au Louvre.

Pour le collectionneur indien, cette lignée importe d'une manière précise : quand les jeux Staunton britanniques atteignirent l'Inde islamique après 1849, la réponse locale — le Staunton musulman — ne fut pas une invention. Ce fut un retour. Le vocabulaire aniconique attendait dans la mémoire persane et moghole depuis mille ans.

Part II — The Indian Court Partie II — La Cour Indienne

Mughal Ivory · Pahari Painting · Kashmiri Lacquer Ivoire Moghol · Peinture Pahari · Laque du Cachemire

When chess returned to India from Persia in the 13th century, it found a continent already shaped by indigenous craft. Mughal courts in Agra and Delhi, the Pahari hill courts, the Lucknow nawabs, and the lacquer ateliers of Srinagar each gave chess their own face. These are the styles that predate British arrival — and the ones a serious collector should chase before colonial-era pieces. Lorsque les échecs revinrent en Inde depuis la Perse au XIIIe siècle, ils trouvèrent un continent déjà façonné par l'artisanat indigène. Les cours mogholes d'Agra et de Delhi, les cours pahari des montagnes, les nababs de Lucknow et les ateliers de laque de Srinagar donnèrent chacun aux échecs un visage propre. Ce sont les styles qui précèdent l'arrivée britannique — ceux qu'un collectionneur sérieux devrait chasser avant les pièces de l'époque coloniale.

III

16th–18th c. · Agra · Delhi · Lahore · Mughal Empire

Mughal Court Chess — Ivory of the Padshah Échecs de la Cour Moghole — L'Ivoire du Padshah

Akbar played, Aurangzeb collected, and Mughal court ateliers produced ivory sets whose elephants and chariots are the direct ancestors of every "Indian-figural" set you'll see at auction. Akbar jouait, Aurangzeb collectionnait, et les ateliers de cour mogholes produisaient des jeux d'ivoire dont les éléphants et les chars sont les ancêtres directs de tout jeu « figuratif indien » que vous verrez aux enchères.

The Mughal emperors did not simply play chess — they curated it. Akbar (r. 1556–1605), according to Abu'l Fazl's Ain-i-Akbari, was such an enthusiast that he had a courtyard at Fatehpur Sikri laid out as a giant board, with palace women dressed in costume serving as the pieces. (The "pachisi court" you can still walk through today is the same idea, applied to a related game.) His son Jahangir, his grandson Shah Jahan and his great-grandson Aurangzeb all kept chess masters at court, and the imperial workshops — the kārkhānas — produced ivory sets to match.

What did a Mughal court set look like? Here is where the modern collector has to do mental work: the canonical "Indian figural" template — Raja and Vizier on howdah-elephants, knights as horse-and-rider, rooks as chariots, bishops as camels-with-rider — is essentially a Mughal template. Every Berhampur, Vizagapatam and "John Company" set of the 19th century is iterating on a vocabulary that the Mughal ateliers fixed in the 16th–17th centuries. The Bishop-as-camel is particularly diagnostic: in Persian shatranj the piece was called fil (elephant), but Mughal carvers, perhaps to differentiate from the Rook-elephant, gave it a camel.

Genuine Mughal-period sets are extraordinarily rare. The most important survivor is the 17th-century ivory set in the Cleveland Museum of Art (catalogue no. 1942.1086), with deeply carved kings on lotus-throne elephants and elaborate howdahs — the standard against which everything else is judged. The National Museum in Delhi holds a similar set attributed to the Deccan. The V&A in London has fragments. The Khalili Collection in London holds Mughal pieces of disputed dating. That is, more or less, the corpus.

For the 2026 collector, this means two things. First: when a London or New York auction catalogue says "Mughal, 17th c." in its description, do not believe it without provenance. Second: the genuine market for "Mughal-style" sets is in the Pahari and Rajput hill-court output of the 18th century — Punjab Hills sets of polychromed ivory, Kishangarh-style miniatures with chess motifs — which can occasionally be acquired through specialised dealers (Spink, Rossi & Rossi, Francesca Galloway) at the £15,000–£60,000 level. Below that price point you are buying 19th-century Berhampur or Vizagapatam, which is a different (and equally beautiful) thing.

Les empereurs moghols ne se contentaient pas de jouer aux échecs — ils les curataient. Akbar (r. 1556–1605), selon l'Ain-i-Akbari d'Abu'l Fazl, était tel passionné qu'il fit aménager une cour à Fatehpur Sikri en échiquier géant, avec les femmes du palais en costume servant de pièces. Son fils Jahangir, son petit-fils Shah Jahan et son arrière-petit-fils Aurangzeb gardèrent tous des maîtres d'échecs à la cour, et les ateliers impériaux — les kārkhānas — produisirent des jeux d'ivoire à la mesure.

À quoi ressemblait un jeu de cour moghole ? C'est ici que le collectionneur moderne doit faire un travail mental : le modèle canonique « figuratif indien » — Raja et Vizir sur éléphants à howdah, cavaliers comme cheval-et-cavalier, tours comme chars, fous comme chameaux-avec-cavalier — est essentiellement un modèle moghol. Chaque jeu de Berhampur, de Vizagapatam et « John Company » du XIXe siècle itère sur un vocabulaire que les ateliers moghols fixèrent aux XVIe–XVIIe siècles.

Les jeux authentiquement de la période moghole sont extraordinairement rares. Le survivant le plus important est le jeu d'ivoire du XVIIe siècle au Cleveland Museum of Art, avec des rois profondément sculptés sur éléphants à trône-lotus et des howdahs élaborés — la référence par rapport à laquelle tout le reste est jugé. Le National Museum de Delhi détient un jeu similaire attribué au Deccan. La V&A à Londres a des fragments. Tel est, peu ou prou, le corpus.

Pour le collectionneur de 2026, cela signifie deux choses. Premièrement : quand un catalogue de Londres ou New York dit « moghol, XVIIe siècle » dans sa description, ne le croyez pas sans provenance. Deuxièmement : le marché authentique pour les jeux « de style moghol » se trouve dans la production des cours pahari et rajput du XVIIIe siècle — jeux des Punjab Hills en ivoire polychromé, miniatures de style Kishangarh à motifs d'échecs — accessibles occasionnellement chez des marchands spécialisés (Spink, Rossi & Rossi, Francesca Galloway) entre 15 000 et 60 000 £.

IV

18th c. · Delhi · Lucknow · Awadh Court

Delhi & Lucknow — The Nawabi Style Delhi et Lucknow — Le Style Nawabi

After Aurangzeb's death the Mughal centre weakened, but Lucknow's nawabs filled the vacuum with a court culture that turned chess into theatre. This is where the figural style hits its baroque peak. The Kashmir Valley sits at the cultural seam between Mughal India and Persianate Central Asia, and its decorative arts have always reflected that. A typical Kashmiri set has pieces turned from a papier-mâché core (sometimes wood-cored), built up in layers, sealed, sanded smooth as eggshell, then painted with a fine brush in the gul-i-andar-gul ("flower within flower") tradition. Kashmir-style sets had a second life in the 20th century as Anglo-Indian "souvenir" production — late 19th and early 20th century examples were exported via Bombay and Madras to British buyers. Three things to know as a collector. First: condition is everything. Lacquer chips, papier-mâché crushes. A "complete" set with even minor surface losses on three or four pieces is essentially worthless to a serious collector. Second: the gilding tells you the date. The "Lund" pattern (sometimes called "Calcutta" or "pre-Staunton Anglo-Indian" by dealers) is one of the great quiet stories of 19th-century chess design. William Lund — a chess-and-mathematical-instrument maker at 24 Cornhill in the City of London from approximately 1820 to 1860 — became the period's most prestigious importer of fine ivory chess sets, contracting their production in Calcutta and Madras to specifications drawn up in London. The result is a set that reads as English in the cabinet but Indian in the workshop.

The Lund pattern is austere, abstract, and tall. The king is a slender pillar with a Maltese-cross or fleur-de-lys finial; the queen is a tall pillar topped with a tiered crown; bishops are mitred but otherwise smooth columns; knights are minimally suggested horse-heads on slender necks (well before Staunton stylised them); rooks are simple battlemented turrets; pawns are graduated columns. When the Staunton pattern became globally dominant after 1849, it presented a problem for Muslim Indian patrons in the Punjab and the Delhi-Lahore-Lucknow corridor. The standard Staunton bishop is mitred — explicitly Christian iconography. The standard Staunton knight is a horse-head, which most jurists consider acceptable but some hardline ones do not. For a Muslim collector or chess club in 1880, the Staunton was simultaneously the most desirable form (because it had become the international tournament standard, used by the strongest masters) and a form that needed alteration to be religiously acceptable.

Punjabi ivory turners — particularly in Lahore (then in undivided Punjab, today in Pakistan) and Karachi — solved this elegantly. They produced what dealers now call "Muslim Staunton" sets: the proportions, weight, and approximate forms of a Staunton, but with the bishop's mitre replaced by a smooth-domed Persianate cap, the king's cross replaced by a simple ball-and-spike finial, the knight reduced to a more abstract horse-head with no anatomical detail, and frequently the use of Arabic numerals or Persian script discreetly marking the squares of the accompanying board. These sets are the rarest of the affordable Indian chess types. Chess reached Burma sometime in the 8th or 9th century, probably overland through Bengal and Arakan, and bedded into the local culture as sit-tu-yin ("four-position chess") — a variant in which both armies are placed on the board only after each player has arranged his own pieces in private behind a curtain, then the curtain is lifted and the game begins. Burmese ivory chess sets from the 19th century have a visual signature that is unmistakable once you have seen one. The minor pieces — knights, bishops, rooks — are turned in tall slender forms with carved finials in floral or animal motifs. Many sets feature the rook as a stylised elephant carrying a small howdah, like Indian sets, but the howdah is recognisably South-East Asian rather than Mughal in proportion.

Two practical points for the collector. First: Burmese sets often have more pieces than 32 — sit-tu-yin uses different opening configurations, and a complete antique Burmese set may include extra pieces or a separate "starting" set. A Burmese set with surface cracking is normal; a Burmese set that is pristine and pure-white is suspicious.

Ceylon's chess tradition sits at a remarkable historical crossing. The island had been Buddhist since the 3rd century BCE, Portuguese-controlled from 1505 to 1658, Dutch from 1658 to 1796, and British from 1796 to 1948. Each layer left its mark on the ivory carving traditions of Galle (the Portuguese-era port), Kandy (the Sinhalese royal capital), and Colombo (the British administrative city). What emerged in the 19th century is a recognisably Ceylonese chess style — neither Indian Mughal-figural nor English-abstract, but a hybrid that draws on all of these influences and adds Sinhalese Buddhist iconographic grace notes.

The visual signature of a Sinhalese set: the ivory itself is paler — Sri Lankan elephant ivory has a creamier base tone — and the carving is more delicate, almost more "feminine" in its line, than the heavier Mughal-Indian carving tradition. Forms tend to be tall and slender. Kings often have a stylised lotus-bud finial (a Buddhist motif), queens a more elaborate floral crown. Pawns are sometimes carved in the form of seated meditating figures. Knights, when figural, are carved as horses with elaborate harnesses and saddle-cloths in the style of Kandyan royal cavalry.

The sub-tradition worth knowing is Galle export ivory — a Portuguese-era and continuing-Dutch-era genre of ivory furniture, religious sculpture, and small fine objects (boxes, fan-handles, chess pieces) made specifically for European export. A Galle chess set has a particular visual quality that comes from being designed for Lisbon and Amsterdam cabinets: more restraint than Indian figural, more decoration than English abstract.

For the collector, Sinhalese sets are scarcer at auction than Indian Berhampur or Vizagapatam — production volumes were lower — but prices are similar. Tibetan / Himalayan — The Buddhist Variant Tibétain / Himalayen — La Variante Bouddhique

Chess reached Tibet from India via the Himalayan trade routes — likely through Nepal and Sikkim — sometime between the 8th and 11th centuries. Once there, it was assimilated into a Buddhist cultural framework that subtly reframed the game's iconography. The Tibetan name for chess is chandraki (from the Sanskrit chaturanga, but Buddhicised). The board layout and rules are essentially Indian-Persian, but the pieces themselves became opportunities for Buddhist iconographic instruction.

A typical Tibetan set: kings represented as enthroned Bodhisattvas (often Avalokiteshvara, the bodhisattva of compassion); queens as protectresses or female deities (Tara, in many sets, or sometimes a stylised dakini); bishops as monastic teachers or lamas in robes; knights as either standard horses or — in the most distinctive sets — as the cosmic horse Lung-ta ("wind-horse") that appears on Tibetan prayer flags; rooks as fortified monastery towers (think Potala Palace silhouette in miniature); pawns as monks or attendant figures.

The materials are different from Indian sets. Tibetan ivory was scarce (the Himalayan plateau is not elephant country) so most antique Tibetan sets are wood — usually carved from pradzo (a Himalayan hardwood) or lacquered wood from Bhutan — finished in polychrome with mineral pigments (the same palette as a thangka painting: lapis blue, vermilion, malachite green, gold leaf). The pieces are often heavier and more sculptural than ivory equivalents, and the polychrome can be extraordinarily fine. Some sets have pieces stained on a metal base for stability.

For the 2026 collector this is the rarest of the styles in this atlas. Probably fewer than a hundred genuinely antique (pre-1900) Tibetan sets exist in private hands worldwide. Auctions occur perhaps once every two or three years, generally at Bonhams "Asian Art" or specialist Tibetan dealers (Carlton Rochell, Bonhams Hong Kong). Prices range £8,000–£35,000 for a complete antique set with original board. A modern Bhutanese or Nepali production set in similar style runs £400–£1,200 and is sold honestly as such.
